Community Characteristics and Recycling Performance

Analyzing and predicting dry recycling collection share across New York City's 59 community districts. This collaborative project examines geographic differences, community-level associations, high-risk district identification, and forecasting performance.

Project overview

Understanding differences in dry recycling collection share across NYC

The team examined how dry recycling collection share varies across New York City's 59 community districts, which community characteristics are associated with those differences, how well community characteristics identify lower-share districts, and whether historical patterns can predict future collection share.

Business problem

Dry recycling collection share varies considerably across NYC community districts. Citywide averages can hide local differences, but a lower share does not automatically mean residents recycle less. Refuse volume, housing conditions, composting participation, and collection practices can also affect the measure. The analysis was designed to help identify patterns and guide further investigation without unfairly labeling communities.

Research questions

RQ1: How does recycling collection share vary across NYC's 59 community districts and five boroughs?

RQ2: Which community characteristics are associated with lower recycling collection share?

RQ3: To what extent can community characteristics explain differences and help identify high-risk districts?

RQ4: How accurately can monthly collection share be forecast one year ahead using historical trends and community characteristics?

Data sources and preparation

The project used NYC Department of Sanitation (DSNY) monthly tonnage data, NYC Planning American Community Survey estimates, limited-English-proficiency indicators from the NYC Civic Engagement Commission, and housing indicators from the NYU Furman Center.

The team checked missing records, duplicate keys, and completeness. Missing data was not treated as zero. Geographic boundaries and reporting periods differed across sources, so source alignment and limitations were documented before combining indicators.

Defining the outcome

Dry recycling collection share (%) = 100 × (Paper + MGP) / (Refuse + Paper + MGP), where MGP represents metal, glass, and plastic.

Annual district shares were calculated from summed annual tonnage, not by averaging monthly percentages. Organics were excluded from the dry recycling numerator and denominator.

Important measurement distinction: Dry recycling collection share is a collection-based indicator. It is not the full waste-diversion rate and does not directly measure how much collected material is ultimately recycled or recovered.

Analysis & modeling

Four research questions, using descriptive analysis and predictive models

RQ1 · Geographic differences

District rankings and tonnage-weighted borough comparisons were used to examine variation in 2025 dry recycling collection share across the city.

RQ2 · Community characteristics

Linear regression, ridge regression, and random forest models examined relationships between collection share and five indicators: limited English proficiency, rent-stabilized housing share, NYCHA share, new residential permits per 1,000 residents, and median household income.

RQ3 · High-risk district identification

Logistic regression and random forest classification ranked districts by predicted risk. The top 15 were compared with the 15 districts with the lowest collection shares in the prior year.

RQ4 · Forecasting

Historical monthly data was used to compare ridge regression and gradient boosting for one-month-ahead forecasts, plus gradient boosting, an averaged ensemble, and a Temporal Fusion Transformer (TFT) for year-ahead forecasting.

Forecast evaluation: Models were trained on 2007–2023 data, tuned on 2024, and tested on January 2025 through October 2026 for the monthly forecasting analysis. Year-ahead models forecast 2025 from a December 2024 origin. Forecast errors were compared with a seasonal naïve baseline.

RQ1 · Geographic variation

District collection share ranged from 5.1% in Bronx Community District 2 to 28.4% in Brooklyn Community District 6. Borough-level shares ranged from 13.1% in the Bronx to 19.5% in Staten Island. Most variation occurred within boroughs rather than between them.

RQ2 · Income was the strongest associated characteristic

Median household income was the strongest community characteristic associated with collection share. In the full regression model, R² was 0.68, and a one-standard-deviation increase in income was associated with approximately 4.8 percentage points higher collection share (95% CI: 3.7 to 6.0). The other four characteristics were uncertain after accounting for income.

RQ3 · Historical performance identified low-share districts best

The prior year's 15 lowest-share districts included 14 of the current year's 15 lowest-share districts. Logistic regression identified 11, and random forest identified 10. Historical collection patterns were the strongest signal for identifying persistently low-share districts.

RQ4 · Forecasting results depended on the horizon

For one-month-ahead forecasts, ridge regression and gradient boosting achieved an MAE of about 0.75 percentage points, approximately 20% lower than the seasonal naïve baseline of 0.95. For year-ahead forecasting, gradient boosting with community characteristics achieved the best MAE at 0.87 points, a modest improvement over the 0.91-point baseline.

Interpreting the 2025 forecast gap: Beginning in April 2025, actual collection shares were about one percentage point higher than forecasts. This coincided with the start of mandatory curbside composting enforcement. From April–October 2025 compared with the same months in 2024, compost collection increased 158% and refuse collection decreased 3.7%. The decrease in refuse alone accounts for about half of the one-point increase in collection share. This illustrates how program and collection changes can shift the measure.

Limitations & responsible interpretation

Use the findings to guide questions—not assign blame

The relationships are observational and do not establish causation or explain individual resident behavior. Community indicators use different geographic proxies and reporting periods, which introduces uncertainty. A lower dry recycling collection share should not be interpreted as proof that residents recycle less responsibly.

Collection share can change when refuse tonnage changes, even if the amount of dry recycling remains the same. Changes in composting participation and collection practices can also affect the percentage. The team recommends using results to prioritize further investigation, review local conditions, and support targeted outreach while communicating uncertainty and limitations.

Project takeaway: Dry recycling collection share varied widely across NYC districts. Median household income was the strongest community characteristic associated with the measure, while prior-year performance was most effective for identifying persistently low-share districts.
