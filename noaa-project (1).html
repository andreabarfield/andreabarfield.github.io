Predicting Economic Damage from U.S. Storm Events

Can recorded event characteristics in the NOAA Storm Events Database predict whether a U.S. storm event will result in economic damage? This collaborative project examines events from 2020 through 2025.

Collaborative team project DSC 450 · Capstone Project October 2026 NOAA Storm Events · 2020–2025

Project overview

Researching whether storm characteristics predict recorded economic damage

The project focuses on a specific prediction task: determining whether a storm event has any recorded property or crop damage. The team used NOAA storm-event characteristics available in the dataset to evaluate whether those features contain useful predictive information.

Business problem

Economic damage from severe weather is costly and difficult to anticipate. The team investigated whether information NOAA records about a storm event—before its full impact is known—contains a useful signal for predicting recorded damage.

Research question

Can we predict if a weather event will result in economic damage based on the recorded event characteristics in the NOAA Storm Events Database for U.S. storm events from 2020 through 2025?

Data source and preparation

The team used 410,311 event records from the NOAA National Centers for Environmental Information Storm Events Database, covering January 1, 2020 through December 31, 2025. Of these, 307,902 had known economic outcomes and 102,409 (24.96%) had unknown damage totals. The original 54 columns were cleaned and reduced to 12 modeling fields.

Events with unknown economic outcomes were excluded from modeling rather than being treated as events with zero damage. Among events with known outcomes, 70,299 (22.83%) had recorded damage greater than zero, while 237,603 (77.17%) had recorded $0 damage.

Predictors and target

Predictors: event type, region, season, and storm duration.

Prediction target: whether an event had any recorded property or crop damage.

Models: Logistic Regression and Random Forest.

Model validation: The team split the data by storm episode (EPISODE_ID), rather than by individual row, to reduce leakage from similar records belonging to the same storm system. Average Precision (PR-AUC) was reported alongside ROC-AUC because the positive class represented approximately 23% of events with known outcomes.

Results & findings

Both models performed above the no-skill baseline

Random Forest achieved the strongest overall performance, with a modest improvement over Logistic Regression. The results indicate that the recorded storm characteristics contain useful information for predicting whether an event is associated with economic damage.

What mattered most?

Random Forest feature-importance analysis identified event type as the strongest predictor, followed by region, storm duration, and season. Event type was approximately 2.4 times more important than region in the model.

Additional feature engineering

The team also explored relative intensity, ranking an event against other events of the same type because NOAA measures intensity differently across storm types (for example, hail size versus wind speed). On the subset where this comparison was possible, adding relative intensity increased Average Precision from approximately 0.691 to 0.703. This feature was not applicable to every event type.

Limitations & next steps

Interpreting the results responsibly

The model identifies patterns in historical recorded outcomes; it does not establish that a storm characteristic causes damage or guarantee the outcome of a future storm. NOAA records include unknown economic outcomes, reporting practices can vary, and property and crop damage estimates do not capture every consequence of severe weather.

Future work proposed by the team includes incorporating geographic exposure and vulnerability information, such as FEMA’s National Risk Index, and investigating whether patterns in damage can help predict storm type.
